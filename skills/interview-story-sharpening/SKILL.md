# SKILL: Interview Story Sharpening

**From:** Preparation and Delivery

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader has practice stories that are not as sharp as they could be.

**Practise with people first.** Read it out loud alone, record yourself, then practise with other
people. This step belongs after those, not instead of them. A story is delivered to a person under
mild pressure, and no amount of text feedback simulates that. Use this to tighten a story between
human practice sessions.

## The structure

Three parts: beginning, middle, end. Deliberately simpler than the four to six point frameworks,
because most people have not yet worked out which story they are telling, let alone divided it
into six.

**Tell it chronologically.** The most common failure is starting with the outcome, because the
teller already knows it, then backfilling context. It makes the story hard to follow.

**Beginning: context first.** Enough for the interviewer to understand what the product was and
what the reader's role in it was. A couple of sentences, before anything happens.

**Then the cast.** Standardized titles, not internal ones: manager, teammate, team lead,
stakeholder, mentee, colleague on another team. Industry-specific job titles lose the listener.
Too many characters and they spend the story working out who is who.

**Middle: what happened, in order.** What the project was, what the reader's role was, whether it
succeeded, how it was received, and what changed in response to criticism.

**End: how it resolved and what was learned.** People like stories that wrap up.

The details that carry the most weight: whether it was the reader's idea, how they took feedback,
how they handled setbacks, and what they applied to the next project.

## What this skill returns

**Clarity.** Where a listener would lose the thread. Unexplained context, undefined jargon,
ambiguous pronouns, a character appearing without introduction.

**Structure.** Whether it runs chronologically. Where the outcome leaked early. Whether the
beginning establishes enough before things start happening.

**Pacing.** What is taking too long, and what is being rushed. Usually the setup is too long and
the outcome too short.

**Does it demonstrate the skill it is meant to?** The reader names the skill. This checks whether
the story actually shows it, which is often the gap: a good story about a hard project that
demonstrates nothing in particular.

**Voice flags.** Anything that sounds machine-written or unlike the rest of the story. Also
anything that sounds written rather than spoken, since this is going to be said out loud.

**Tightening suggestions, not a rewrite.** Specific cuts and reorderings the reader applies
themselves.

## The voice caution

This is the point of running a story past anything at all: **do not lose your voice and sound like
AI wrote it.** A story delivered in someone else's register is worse than an unpolished one,
because the interviewer hears the gap between the story and the person telling it.

So: suggestions only, never a rewritten paragraph to memorize. And never read a story out; the
interviewer will hear it in your tone.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are an experienced engineering interviewer giving feedback on a practice story. Do not
> rewrite it. Give me feedback I can apply myself.
>
> Here is my story: [PASTE]. It is meant to demonstrate [SKILL]. My interviewer will be [A
> RECRUITER / ENGINEERING MANAGER / PRODUCT MANAGER / ENGINEER ON THE TEAM].
>
> Tell me whether it has a clear beginning, middle, and end, and whether it runs chronologically or
> gives away the outcome early. Tell me where a listener would lose the thread, where the pacing
> drags, and where I am rushing.
>
> Tell me honestly whether it demonstrates [SKILL], or whether it is just an interesting project.
>
> Flag anything that sounds machine-written, or written rather than spoken.
>
> Give me specific cuts and reorderings. Do not hand me a rewritten version.

## What to tell the reader

- **Humans first.** Practise out loud and with other people before and after this.
- **Ask practice partners to vary.** Friendly and cold, so the reader has met both.
- **Build a bank.** One story per skill the target company names in its values, ready before the
  interview.
- **Connect it to a company goal.** What changed because of this work, traced through to something
  anyone would recognize as mattering.

## What this skill does not do

It does not write stories, conduct mock interviews, or know what a specific company wants. It reads
a draft closely and hands back notes, and it will not put words in the reader's mouth.
