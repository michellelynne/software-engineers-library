# SKILL: Success Statements

**From:** Success Statements Worksheet (Success Statements)

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader has a list of what they did and needs it in language that
travels. Performance review, promotion case, resume, or the question at a skip-level about what
they have been working on.

## The format

> I improved this thing by this measurable amount using this method.

One sentence. No longer than a resume bullet, roughly 180 to 300 characters. Three parts, and
all three are required:

- **The thing improved.** A project, role, feature, or bug.
- **The measurable amount.** The number, estimate, or percentage.
- **The method.** What was actually built, integrated, tested, or fixed.

Worked example:

> I increased the product catalog by 18% and generated $10,000 in pilot sales through an
> automated sync with the new vendor.

## Say this out loud, because the book does

**With this specific format, it is often easier to write these yourself.** The book includes a
prompt and then says plainly that writing them by hand is frequently faster, and that the more
the reader practices the better they get. This is a skill where the honest recommendation is
sometimes not to use the skill.

Use it to get unstuck on a hard one, to process a large backlog, or to see a first pass. Do not
use it as the default for every statement, because the practice is what makes the next quarter
easier, and this is a sentence the reader may have to defend out loud in a review.

## How to run it

**Step 1. Take the raw list,** ideally from the aggregation step rather than memory.

**Step 2. Never invent a number.** Every figure comes from the data provided or a connected
source. A statement with a fabricated percentage is worse than no statement, because the reader
may have to defend it in a room with the person who owns that metric.

**Step 3. Strip the jargon from the method.** The method has to be understandable to anyone at
the organization. They may not know the project names, the internal systems, or the acronyms.
Write for a reader two teams away.

**Step 4. Roll related items up.** Many small statements dilute. One major change carries more
weight than several minor ones, and a project usually deserves one strong statement rather than
five weak ones.

**Step 5. Order by impact.** Most impactful or most interesting first. These get skimmed.

**Step 6. Flag what could not be written and why.** Anything missing a method or a measurable
amount does not become a padded statement. It becomes a line saying which piece is missing, so
the reader knows exactly what to go and find.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are a software engineer compiling work from the last [PERIOD] into success statements.
> Here is the raw data: [PASTE YOUR LIST].
>
> Write success statements in this format: "I improved this thing by this measurable amount
> using this method." A success statement is a single short sentence, no longer than a resume
> bullet, that shows the impact of what I did, in language anyone can understand.
>
> Keep the method free from jargon and never invent data or numbers. Cite each statement from
> the data I provided.
>
> Roll related items into one statement. There can be several for a project, but one major
> change has more impact than multiple minor ones.
>
> Return one statement per line, most impactful or interesting first.
>
> Highlight anything that could not be used because it was missing a piece, such as a method or
> a measurable amount.

## What to tell the reader

- **Estimates are allowed; inventions are not.** "Roughly 10% of users" is honest and usable.
  A precise figure nobody can source is a liability.
- **Numbers may require asking.** Impact data often sits with another team. Let your manager
  know before you go asking around.
- **These compound.** Success statements merge into achievements on a brag sheet, which feed
  reviews, promotion cases, and resumes. Written once each quarter, they are never written under
  pressure.
- **Read it back as one sentence.** If it does not survive being said aloud, it is too long or
  too cluttered.

## What this skill does not do

It does not gather the data, verify the numbers, or decide what was impressive. It converts
evidence into sentences, and it refuses to fill a gap with a guess.
