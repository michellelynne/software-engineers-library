# SKILL: Memo Editing

**From:** Memos

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader has a draft and wants it better. A memo, a status update,
anything going to leadership. The draft has to exist first; this skill does not start one.

## Editor, not writer

The book's position is precise and worth repeating to the reader: it is a better editor than it
is a writer. It can help with grammar, ideation, and shortening. It cannot write a book for
you, but it can tell you when you are writing in the passive voice.

Two reasons the distinction matters here.

**The reader is building a leadership brand, not producing one document.** Memos accumulate into
how people at the organization perceive them. A voice assembled by a tool is not theirs, and the
gap becomes obvious the moment they have to speak to the same material in a meeting.

**It does not know the internal jargon.** Every organization has terms, systems, and shorthand
that do not exist outside it. Language that looks like jargon to an outside editor is often the
precise term the audience expects, and "simplifying" it makes the memo wrong.

So this skill returns suggestions, not a rewrite. The reader applies what they agree with.

## What this skill produces

**Passive voice flags.** Each instance quoted, with an active rewrite offered. Passive voice is
not always wrong, and it is genuinely useful when the actor is unknown or deliberately
de-emphasized. Flag it, say which case this looks like, and let the reader decide.

**Cuts.** Specific sentences and paragraphs that could go, with the reason: repeated, obvious,
or not serving the memo's purpose. Give a target reduction rather than a vague instruction to
tighten.

**Clarity flags.** Places a reader outside the team would stop: an unexplained reference, an
argument with a missing step, a conclusion that does not follow. Say what is unclear and what
question the reader is left with. Do not supply the answer, because only the author has it.

**Structure notes.** Whether the most important thing is near the top. Leadership memos get
skimmed, and the higher up they travel the more they get skimmed.

**What was deliberately left alone.** Name the terms treated as intentional jargon rather than
cut, so the reader can correct that judgment if it was wrong.

## What not to touch

- The author's voice, sentence rhythm, and characteristic phrasing
- Internal terminology, project names, system names
- Anything that would change a claim rather than its wording
- Numbers and factual assertions, which are the author's to stand behind

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are an editor, not a writer. Here is my draft memo: [PASTE]. My audience is [WHO].
>
> Do not rewrite it. Give me suggestions I can apply myself.
>
> Flag every instance of passive voice, quote it, and offer an active version, noting where the
> passive is actually the right choice.
>
> Show me what I can cut, with a reason for each, and tell me roughly what percentage shorter it
> could be.
>
> Tell me where someone outside my team would get lost, and what question they would be left
> with. Do not answer the question, just name it.
>
> Tell me whether the most important point is high enough up.
>
> Assume any term that looks like internal jargon is deliberate, and list what you left alone so
> I can check that judgment.

## What to tell the reader

- **The voice is the asset.** Memos are how leadership comes to know the reader. Sounding like
  everyone else defeats the purpose of writing one.
- **Accept selectively.** Every suggestion is a proposal, and disagreeing with half of them is
  a sign the reader has a voice worth protecting.
- **Passive voice is a flag, not an error.** "The service was restarted" is right when who
  restarted it does not matter.
- **The muscle strengthens.** Each memo edited this way should need fewer flags than the last.

## What this skill does not do

It does not write the memo, check the facts, or know the reader's organization. It reads a draft
closely and hands back a list the author chooses from.
