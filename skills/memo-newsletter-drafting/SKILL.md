# SKILL: Memo Newsletter Drafting

**From:** Newsletter Worksheet (Memo Newsletter)

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader has to tell leadership what their team has been doing and has raw
notes rather than a draft.

## Who reads this and why it matters

**Memos are for management; demos are for users.** This is the memo.

It shows leadership what the team is up to, and it saves them time when their own directors ask
what their teams have produced lately. It also reaches the team, who are pleased to see their work
written down, and it puts the reader's name in front of people who make decisions about
reorganizations and promotions.

**The higher it travels, the more it gets skimmed.** Structure accordingly: the reader's audience
is a director, the director's audience is a VP, and each level reads less.

## The structure

**Key Insights.** Two paragraphs, written so a VP understands them without understanding the
technology, while still being interested in how it got done. This is the only part that is
guaranteed to be read, so it carries the argument.

The register is specific and worth getting right. Not a technical summary simplified, and not
business language with the substance removed. It should explain what changed for the business and
be honest about what made it possible, in a way that survives being repeated by someone who was not
there.

**Impact.** What changed, in terms the organization already measures.

**Highlights.** One line each.

**Lowlights.** One line each, and they belong here. A newsletter with no lowlights reads as
marketing and gets trusted accordingly. Including what did not work is what makes the highlights
credible.

**Key investments.** Where the team's effort went that has not paid off yet. This is where tech
debt, platform work, and everything else invisible from the outside becomes legible.

## Roll up before sorting

If several features affected the same result, combine them into one item with the combined number
rather than listing each. Rolled-up data is both more impressive and more honest than the same
result reported four times.

## Two things the draft should tell the reader

**Which items are most interesting and exciting,** so the reader knows what to lead with. The
person closest to the work is usually the worst judge of which part is remarkable to an outsider.

**Which claim needs more information.** Anything stated as an outcome without evidence behind it.
Better to find that now than to be asked about it by a VP.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are a director of engineering who reads dozens of team newsletters.
>
> Here are my raw notes from this time period: [PASTE]. My team is [TEAM AND WHAT IT DOES].
>
> Draft the Key Insights section as two paragraphs a VP would understand without needing to
> understand the technology, but is interested in how it got done.
>
> If multiple features affected the same result, roll that data up, then sort the rest into
> highlights, lowlights, and key investments as one-line bullets.
>
> Tell me which items are the most interesting and exciting, and which claim needs more
> information.

## What to tell the reader

- **Edit it into your own voice.** This is going out under the reader's name, repeatedly, and it
  accumulates into how leadership perceives them.
- **Keep the lowlights.** They are what make the rest believable.
- **Send it regularly.** A newsletter that appears at review time is visible as what it is.
- **Never invent a number.** This is read by people who may have the real one.

## What this skill does not do

It does not send anything, gather the underlying data, or verify claims. It turns notes into a
draft aimed at the people who will actually read it.
