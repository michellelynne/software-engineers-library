# SKILL: Brag Sheet Compiler

**From:** Brag Sheet Worksheet

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader has success statements accumulated and needs them to become
something a manager can act on. Review season, a promotion case, or a new manager who has no idea
what the reader has been doing.

## What a brag sheet is for

Three audiences, and they want different things: performance reviews, promotions, and
introductions to new managers or anyone curious what the reader has been up to.

The mechanism it feeds is real. Someone whose impact is always documented and ready never has to
reconstruct a year of work from memory under deadline, and when management is assessing teams,
they are the person whose contribution is legible.

## Three records, not thirty

The compression is the work. Several quarters of success statements become **at most three records
of achievement, one to two sentences each.**

Three because that is what a manager can carry into a room and repeat. A list of thirty
accomplishments communicates that the reader was busy. Three achievements communicate what they
are good at.

So this is a selection problem before it is a writing problem. Pick the most impressive, group
related work under a single record, and let the smaller items sit underneath as supporting
evidence rather than competing for attention.

**Keep the success statements behind each record.** The record is the claim; the statements are
the evidence. When a manager asks what the reader means by a record, the statements are the answer.

## Write it to make the case

The framing is explicit: written to show the reader should get the maximum available raise. That is
not an invitation to inflate. Every record still traces to success statements built from real data,
and the strength comes from selection and framing rather than invention.

What that means in practice: lead with outcome rather than activity, use the organization's own
language for what matters, and make the scope visible. "Led" and "contributed to" are different
claims and the reader should be able to defend whichever one appears.

## Then the goals

A brag sheet is not only backward-looking. Based on the reader's current role and the progress the
records show, suggest goals to strive for next, inside the organization or in the wider tech
community. For each goal, concrete steps.

The progress monitor pairs with this: each step, the goal it serves, and where it currently stands.
A class, a project, a book, a talk. This is what turns a review conversation from a defence of the
past into a plan.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are an engineering manager reviewing documentation for raises and promotions for your team.
>
> Here are my success statements from the last four quarters: [PASTE].
>
> Group them into no more than three records of achievement, each one to two sentences. Choose the
> most impressive, written to show I should get the maximum available raise. Keep the success
> statements that support each record listed underneath it.
>
> Based on my current role and progress, suggest goals I should strive for next, either within my
> organization or in the tech community. For each of those goals, suggest concrete steps I can take
> to achieve them.
>
> Do not invent anything that is not in my statements. If a record would be stronger with a number
> I have not given you, tell me what to go and find.

## What to tell the reader

- **Defend every word.** This gets read in a room where the reader may be asked to expand on any
  line.
- **Update it quarterly.** A brag sheet written once a year is written from memory, and memory
  favours the recent.
- **Watch what is missing.** Work that cannot become a record may be work that does not show
  leadership. If it cannot go on the brag sheet, it may not be the right task.
- **Bring the goals.** Arriving with a plan changes the nature of the conversation.

## What this skill does not do

It does not gather the evidence, verify the numbers, negotiate, or predict the outcome. It turns
accumulated statements into an argument the reader can stand behind.
