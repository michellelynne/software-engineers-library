# SKILL: Regional Benefit Benchmarking

**From:** Company Filters (Find Your Dream Pretty Good Job Worksheet)

## When to load this skill

Load this skill when the reader needs a market range for a benefit before they can set their
own acceptable range. Also load it when they are comparing two locations, or wondering whether
an offer is normal.

## Why region drives this

Many companies use cost of labor to decide the minimum they can offer, so the same title at
the same company pays and benefits differently by location. Seniority moves the number a
second time. A range quoted without both is not usable.

## Required inputs

Ask for all three before answering. Do not guess any of them.

1. **The benefit.** Salary, bonus, equity, 401(k) or pension match, PTO, parental leave,
   healthcare contribution, learning budget, home office stipend, on-call compensation,
   sabbatical, and so on.
2. **The region.** Metro area is better than country. "Austin" beats "United States."
3. **Years of experience,** or the target level if the reader knows it.

## What this skill produces

**A typical range,** stated as a low, a middle, and a high, with the unit named explicitly
(percent of salary, days per year, dollars per year, weeks).

**A note on what moves it,** covering the two levers separately:
- how this region compares to the national or regional baseline, and why
- how the range shifts between mid-level and senior

**A recency statement.** Say what period the figures reflect and how quickly this particular
benefit tends to change. Healthcare contributions and salary move faster than PTO policy.

**A side-by-side comparison** when the reader names two regions. Same benefit, same experience
level, two columns, plus one line on which difference is real and which is cost-of-labor
arithmetic that a remote role might erase.

**A verification step,** always. Name two or three places the reader can check the number
against reality: current job listings that publish ranges, salary data sites, the company's
own benefits page, and people in their network who work there.

## Prompt the reader can run directly

> You are a compensation research assistant. I am a software engineer with [N] years of
> experience, based in [REGION]. I want to understand the market range for [BENEFIT].
>
> Give me a typical low, middle, and high for that benefit, with the unit stated. Then explain
> how [REGION] compares to the broader market for this benefit, and how the range changes
> between a mid-level and a senior engineer.
>
> Tell me what period your figures reflect and how fast this benefit tends to change.
>
> Then list the specific sources I should check the number against before I rely on it.

For a comparison, add:

> Now run the same benefit for [SECOND REGION] and put the two side by side.

## What to tell the reader

- **Treat every number as a starting point.** Benefit data is patchy, self-reported, and
  ages quickly. Sanity-check against real listings and real salary data before setting a range
  you would walk away over.
- **Set the range, not the target.** The worksheet asks for an acceptable range so that a
  package can be compared as a whole. A company landing low on one benefit should land high on
  another.
- **Blank is a valid answer.** If a benefit is not relevant to the reader or to where they
  live, leave it blank and do not use it as a filter.
- **Three filters, not ten.** This skill feeds the ranking step. The reader picks the three
  benefits that matter most, and those become the filters applied to the company list.

## What this skill does not do

It does not tell the reader what they are worth, does not negotiate, and does not evaluate a
specific offer against a specific company. It establishes what normal looks like so the reader
can decide what is acceptable to them.
